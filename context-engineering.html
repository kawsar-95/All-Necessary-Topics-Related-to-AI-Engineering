Context window budgeting

A model has a fixed-size working memory. Every byte you allocate competes with every other byte. Context engineering is the discipline of deciding what lives where — system prompt, history, tool definitions, retrieved docs, prior outputs, the user's actual request.

The budget model

Treat the context window as a finite pie. Five claimants, each with a minimum allocation and a cost-per-token that varies:

A 200K-token window broken into system + tools + retrieval + history + current turn. Percentages are typical for an agentic RAG app, not universal.

Allocation principles

  • → System prompt: floor of ~1–2K tokens. Persona + schema + hard constraints. Larger prompts cost more per call AND dilute attention.[14]
  • → Tool definitions: floor of ~1–3K tokens. Only include tools the current step might call; load specialised tools lazily.
  • → Retrieval: budget = window − system − tools − current turn − reserved output. Cap retrieval chunks; prefer quality over quantity.
  • → History: shrink aggressively. Use summarization (§2) or selective replay to keep it bounded.
  • → Output reservation: always reserve enough tokens for the response. Forcing truncation produces silent failures.
# A budget calculator
def budget(window: int, tools: list[dict], retrieved: str,
          system: str, history: str, max_out: int) -> dict:
    return {
        "system":      len(enc.encode(system)),
        "tools":       sum(len(enc.encode(json.dumps(t))) for t in tools),
        "retrieval":   len(enc.encode(retrieved)),
        "history":     len(enc.encode(history)),
        "max_output":  max_out,
        "total":       sum((len(enc.encode(x)) for x in [system, history, retrieved])) + max_out,
        "headroom":    window - "total",
    }
The "Lost in the Middle" effect. Liu et al. (2023) showed that models reliably recall information at the beginning and end of long contexts but perform close to random in the middle.[1] This is the single most important budget heuristic: put the things you most need retrieved in the start or the end of your prompt, not buried in the middle. (§7 expands this.)
📐Layman's versionplain English, no jargon

Imagine the AI's brain is a desk, and you have to fit everything on it.

The desk is a fixed size — 8K pieces of paper, or 200K, depending on the model. Every instruction, every chat history message, every retrieved document, every tool definition takes up space on that desk. If you cram too much in, papers slide off the edges and the AI can't see them. Worse, the papers in the middle of the pile get forgotten, even if they're still there.

The five big claimants of desk space:

1. System stuff — the job description, the rules, the output format. Small but critical. Don't bloat it.
2. Tool definitions — descriptions of what tools the AI can use. Only include the ones it might actually need right now.
3. Retrieved documents — usually the biggest chunk. Five doc snippets from your knowledge base. Trim ruthlessly.
4. Chat history — old messages. Summarize old ones, drop the noise.
5. Current request + answer — what the user just asked, room for the AI to respond.

The killer insight from research: if you put the most important papers in the middle of the pile, the AI forgets them. It pays strong attention to the top of the pile and the bottom of the pile, but the middle is fuzzy. So always put the most critical information at the start or the end of the prompt.

Production rule: the biggest slice of the budget usually goes to retrieved documents. If you're running over budget, shrink the retrieval first — not the system prompt. Retrieval bloat is the #1 silent cost in production AI apps.

The desk analogy: your AI's context window is a desk with a fixed surface area. Five piles compete for space: rules, tools, references, history, current task. The top of the desk is "in focus" and the middle of any pile gets fuzzy. Always put the must-read items at the very top of the most visible pile — don't bury them. When the desk is overflowing, the first thing to trim is the reference pile, not the rules.

Conversation summarization & rolling memory

A 50-turn chat is 50× the size of a 1-turn chat. Most of those tokens are noise — greetings, "can you clarify," half-finished thoughts. Summarization trades raw history for compressed state, keeping the model coherent without the linear cost.

When to summarize

Three triggers, in increasing aggressiveness:

  1. Token-budget triggered: when history > N tokens (e.g. 4K), compress the oldest chunk into a summary block.
  2. Turn-count triggered: every K turns, summarise the earliest K−1 into one block. Predictable, simpler.
  3. Semantic triggered: summarise when the topic shifts (detected via an embedding similarity drop, or when a new user message is far from recent history).

The summarization pattern

Rolling memory: oldest turns are condensed by the model into a summary block, recent turns stay verbatim. Sliding window plus summarization.

What to keep in the summary

A naive "summarise the conversation" produces mush. Use a structured schema:

# A summary schema that survives summarisation well
{
  "user_goal":         "<one sentence>",
  "decisions_made":    ["<list of conclusions reached>"],
  "open_questions":    ["<list of unanswered items>"],
  "key_facts_learned": ["<facts the user told us>"],
  "entities":          ["<names, ids, URLs the user mentioned>"],
  "user_preferences":  ["<style, format, tone preferences expressed>"],
  "tool_results_to_remember": ["<non-recreatable values>"]
}
Hierarchical summarisation — the technique Anthropic's prompt-engineering docs recommend: keep a rolling summary of the very oldest turns, a mid-resolution summary of the middle turns, and the recent turns verbatim. This preserves both long-arc continuity and recent detail.[14]

Information loss is real

Summarisation is lossy compression. Decisions, exact numbers, and quoted text are the first things to disappear. Mitigation:

  • Extract key facts into a separate structured slot that survives summarization.
  • Use the summary as index, not source of truth. When a detail matters, retrieve from the original log.
  • For high-stakes conversations, persist verbatim transcripts to a DB even after summarising into context.

Imagine a 4-hour meeting you're trying to summarise into 5 bullet points.

If you keep every word of every conversation, the AI's desk fills up fast. A 50-turn chat eats tons of tokens. Most of those words are noise: greetings, false starts, "what do you mean?", people repeating themselves. The actual important stuff — the decisions, the facts, the preferences — is maybe 5% of the words.

Summarization is the trick: every K turns, ask the AI to compress the oldest K turns into a tight summary. Keep the recent turns verbatim. The summary gets injected back at the front of the conversation as if it were the first thing you said.

The key is asking the AI to summarize in a structured way. Don't just say "summarise the chat" — you'll get mush. Instead, say: "extract: what the user is trying to do, what decisions we made, what questions are still open, what facts they mentioned, what names/IDs came up, what preferences they expressed." This structured summary is much more useful than a paragraph — the AI can later look at it and find exactly what it needs.

The catch: summarization is lossy. Like compressing a photo — you save space, but you lose detail. Numbers drift. Exact quotes disappear. If something matters a lot, pull it out separately before you summarise and put it in a "key facts" slot. Don't trust a summary to be the source of truth.

The best production trick: hierarchical summarization. Keep a tight summary of the very oldest stuff, a mid-resolution summary of the middle, and the recent turns verbatim. Like an executive briefing: one page, then three pages, then the full transcript. The AI can ask for the right level of detail as needed.

The meeting-notes analogy: you can't keep every word of a 4-hour meeting on your desk, but you do need to know what was decided. A good meeting summary lists the decisions, action items, and key facts — not a transcript. Same with chat history: keep a "decisions + facts + questions" summary of old turns, and the recent turns verbatim. The AI gets the same comprehension for a fraction of the desk space.

Prompt caching strategies

Frontier providers let you cache a prefix of your prompt: identical tokens at the start of a request cost dramatically less and run faster. Used well, caching drops 80–90% of the input bill for apps with stable system prompts and long retrieval sections.

How caching works (Anthropic + OpenAI + Gemini)

ProviderMechanismCostTTLMin cache size
Anthropic Auto-cache of system + tools + first user message; explicit cache_control breakpoints for custom control 5–10× cheaper on cache hit (write pricing per tonne) 5 min (refreshes on hit) 1024 tokens (Claude 4 Sonnet), 4096 (Haiku)
OpenAI Auto-cache on prompts >1024 tokens matching a recent prefix 50% off cached tokens 5–10 min (sliding window) 1024 tokens
Gemini Explicit cachedContent resource ~75% off (free for system+prompt, regular per-token for output) Configurable, default ~60 min 2048 tokens (Flash), 4096 (Pro)

Anthropic's cache breakpoints are the most flexible: you mark up to 4 ephemeral blocks within a prompt. Anything before the breakpoint is cached; anything after is rebuilt each call. This lets you cache system + tools + retrieved docs (system), and pass only the new turn's user message.[15]

The standard cache layout

Cache the prefix that doesn't change (system + tools + stable retrieval), pass only the new user message after the breakpoint.

What to cache vs what not to

Cache aggressively

  • System prompt (persona + rules)
  • Tool schemas (when tools are stable)
  • Large retrieved docs that are reused across turns
  • Few-shot examples (if they're static)
  • Long persona descriptions, style guides

Don't cache

  • The user's most recent message (changes every turn)
  • Retrieval that changes every call (e.g., today's news)
  • Token-by-token streaming prefixes (cache invalidates)
  • Anything where freshness matters per-call

The cost math

For an agent with a 60K-token system + tools prefix and a 4K-token user message:

  • → No cache: 64K input tokens × price_per_m
  • → With cache: 60K cached (≈10× cheaper) + 4K uncached
  • → Effective savings: ~85% on input costs for the steady state
Cache invalidation is the gotcha. Even a single new character in the cached region busts the cache for everything after. Change a version number, fix a typo, even add a dynamic timestamp — cache miss. Cache control means also keeping the cached prefix byte-identical across calls. Hash your system prompt in CI; alert if it drifts.[16][17] 
# Anthropic prompt caching with explicit breakpoints
messages = [
    {"role": "system", "content": [
        {"type": "text", "text": SYSTEM_PROMPT,
         "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}},
    ]},
    {"role": "user", "content": [
        {"type": "text", "text": tool_docs_str,
         "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}},
        {"type": "text", "text": f"<retrieved>{retrieved}</retrieved>"},
        {"type": "text", "text": f"Question: {user_q}"},  # not cached
    ]},
]
# Inspect the response.usage to verify cache hit:
# response.usage.cache_read_input_tokens > 0  → cache hit
💾Layman's versionplain English, no jargon

Imagine if you had to rewrite the same long instruction manual every time a new customer walked in.

That's what most LLM apps do — they send the full system prompt + tool descriptions + retrieved context + the user's question every single call. Even if nothing changed from the last call. You're paying the AI company to re-read the same 50,000 words over and over.

Prompt caching is the fix. All the major AI providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Gemini) now let you mark a portion of your prompt as "cached" — meaning if the next call has the same prefix, they reuse the work instead of re-reading it. Cost drops 80–90%. Latency drops too.

The trick: structure your prompt so the stable parts come at the start (system prompt, tool descriptions, retrieved documents you reuse), and the changing parts come at the end (the user's question). Cache the start, send the end fresh each time.

The landmine: the cached prefix has to be byte-identical across calls. Change one character — even a typo fix, even a version number, even a timestamp that updates per call — and the cache misses, costing you full price. So:

• Put anything dynamic (timestamps, request IDs, today's date) AFTER the cache breakpoint.
• Hash your system prompt in CI; alert if it changes unexpectedly.
• Be careful with retrieved documents — if the top result changes between calls, the whole cached block after that point is invalid.

Real savings: for a customer-support agent with a 60K-token system prompt and a 2K user message, caching the 60K means you pay ~10× less on every call after the first. For a million-call-per-day app, this is the difference between bankruptcy and a healthy margin.

The textbook analogy: every LLM call is like making a student read a 50-page textbook to answer one question. Even if the textbook is the same every time, they re-read the whole thing. Prompt caching is letting the student keep the textbook open on their desk and only ask new questions — the textbook stays put, they only spend time on the new answer. Massive savings, but only if the textbook literally doesn't change.

Hierarchical memory

Humans don't have one type of memory — they have working memory, short-term, episodic, and procedural layers. AI systems that aspire to long-running tasks need the same stratification. Hierarchical memory = store different kinds of state at different timescales, summarised at each layer.

The four-layer model

Four-layer hierarchical memory. Each layer trades size for attention: working memory is small but always attended; external storage is unbounded but only retrieved on demand.
LayerWhat lives thereWhere it livesUpdate cadence
Working memory The current prompt: system + tools + retrieved + current request API request payload Every call (ephemeral)
Short-term Recent turns of this session, recent tool results, recent decisions Session store (DB row keyed by session_id) Each turn, with rolling summarization
Long-term User preferences, known facts about the user, project context Vector store + structured DB keyed by user_id Slow: when explicit facts are extracted
Episodic Distilled "memories" of past sessions, lessons learned, what worked Vector store keyed by user_id; written by a memory-update agent End of session, or when triggered
External / tools The world: documents, code, databases, calendars, anything retrievable Your DBs, RAG indices, search APIs Retrieved on demand

The MemGPT insight

Packer et al. (2023) proposed treating the LLM's context like virtual memory: the model itself decides when to evict information from working memory to external storage, and when to page it back in. The model uses tool calls (recall, archival_search) to manipulate its own memory.[2] This is the architectural blueprint for every serious long-running AI agent in 2024–2025.[14]

The memory-update pattern

# An end-of-session memory consolidation step
def consolidate_memory(transcript: str, user_id: str):
    extracted = llm.extract_facts(transcript, schema={
        "user_preferences": [...],
        "project_facts": [...],
        "episodic_lessons": [...],
    })
    for fact in extracted.episodic_lessons:
        embedding = embed(fact.text)
        vector_store.upsert(user_id, embedding, fact.text)
    for pref in extracted.user_preferences:
        user_db.merge_pref(user_id, pref)   # structured update
Memory decay and contradiction. Long-term memory stores drift over time — a fact from a previous session may be contradicted by the user's current stance. Always tag memories with timestamps and a confidence score; surface potentially-stale memories for user confirmation, or weight them by recency at retrieval.[10]
🧠Layman's versionplain English, no jargon

Humans don't have one type of memory. AI shouldn't either.

Your brain has layers: working memory (what you're thinking about right now), short-term (what you did today), long-term (your name, your preferences, your childhood), and episodic (specific memories like "the trip to Paris in 2019"). Different timescales, different uses.

Long-running AI agents need the same:

1. Working memory: what's in the prompt right now — system instructions, current user message, retrieved docs. Fast, expensive, but always "in focus."
2. Short-term memory: the last few turns of THIS conversation. In a database, loaded when needed.
3. Long-term memory: facts about the user that should persist across sessions — their name, role, preferences, projects. Retrieved on demand.
4. Episodic memory: summaries of past sessions. Useful for continuity.
5. External: documents, code, databases, anything the AI can look up.

The breakthrough paper "MemGPT" showed that the AI itself should manage its own memory: it should be allowed to "evict" old facts from working memory into external storage, and "page them back in" when needed. The AI decides what's important enough to remember and what to forget. Same as humans.

Production gotcha: memories contradict themselves. Last Tuesday you preferred bullet points; today you prefer prose. Don't just trust long-term memory blindly — tag facts with timestamps, surface them for confirmation, or weight by recency.

The memory-walk analogy: working memory is the desk in front of you. Short-term memory is the notebook from yesterday's meeting. Long-term memory is your address and your spouse's name. Episodic memory is that vacation you took in 2019. External storage is the library. A good AI — like a good person — knows which layer to look in, doesn't overload the desk with old news, and checks the library when a specific fact is needed.

Context compression & pruning

When summarization is too lossy and you have no time to spare, compression removes redundancy and pruning drops irrelevant tokens. The goal: same information density, fewer tokens.

Compression techniques

TechniqueHow it worksCompression ratioUse case
LLM summarisation Ask the model to summarise; structured schema for fact preservation 5–20× Conversation history, long documents
LLMLingua Small classifier prunes low-information words from prompts 5–20× Latency-sensitive inference, prompt compression for cost
Selective context Use self-information (perplexity) to drop uninformative phrases 5× RAG context pruning
Embedding-based filtering Score each chunk by relevance to the query, drop low-score chunks Variable RAG with many retrieved docs
Token-budget knapsack Greedy: include chunks in relevance order until budget is filled Variable RAG with strict context caps
Deduplication Near-duplicate detection (cosine > 0.95), keep one copy 1.5–3× RAG over a corpus with overlapping content

LLMLingua — the canonical compression model

LLMLingua (Microsoft, 2023) trains a small classifier to predict which tokens are informative given the prompt; non-informative tokens are dropped. It can compress prompts 5–20× while preserving the LLM's ability to answer accurately.[3]

LLMLingua and similar tools identify low-information phrases ("the capital of Bangladesh") and drop them while preserving the meaning.

Pruning: surgical removal of irrelevant context

Compression shrinks everything; pruning removes parts that aren't relevant to the current task:

  • Relevance pruning: score each retrieved chunk by similarity to the user's question; drop the bottom quartile.
  • Recency pruning: drop turns older than N if they've been incorporated into a summary.
  • Task-aware pruning: for tool calls, drop tool definitions not used by the current step.
  • Diversity pruning: when multiple retrieved docs cover the same fact, keep the highest-scoring one.

The combined approach

  1. Retrieve K=20 candidate chunks (cheap, fast).
  2. Re-rank with a cross-encoder (precise).
  3. Prune to top 5 by relevance (drop redundancy).
  4. Compress each chunk with LLMLingua if still over budget.
  5. Inject into the prompt in a marked section (§7 — Lost in the Middle).
The "compression + pruning" combo. Compress everywhere, prune where it hurts least. Pruning preserves information density; compression reduces absolute size. They're complementary. The Anthropic context-engineering playbook recommends both, in that order, before any prompt goes to the model.[14]
✂️Layman's versionplain English, no jargon

Two ways to make a long document shorter.

Compression is summarization with a finer tool. "The user is asking about the weather in Dhaka, which is the capital of Bangladesh, a country in South Asia..." → "weather in Dhaka." Same meaning, 60% fewer words. Tools like LLMLingua use a small AI to find the parts of a sentence that aren't carrying information and cut them. Like a journalist's red pen that knows which words are filler.

Pruning is different — it doesn't rewrite, it deletes. You retrieved 20 documents to answer a question. 5 of them are relevant. Drop the other 15 entirely. No rewriting, just removal. This is how you handle huge RAG contexts: bring back a thousand candidates, then ruthlessly cut.

The best production recipe combines both: retrieve 20 candidates → re-rank to top 5 → drop redundant ones → if still over budget, compress each with LLMLingua → inject into the prompt in the right place. Each step saves tokens; together they keep you under budget without losing the parts that matter.

The trade-off: compression and pruning are lossy. You save space, but you lose detail. A fact that gets compressed away might have been the answer. The art is knowing when to compress and when to keep things verbatim — usually you compress noise (greetings, repetitions) and keep signal (facts, decisions, names).

The book-editor analogy: compression is a copy editor cutting filler ("the capital of Bangladesh, which is in South Asia" → "Dhaka, Bangladesh") — every word earns its place. Pruning is the same editor ripping out whole chapters that don't relate to the current story. Both make the book tighter; both lose some information. A great editor does both — keeps the story, drops the fluff.

Tool result truncation strategies

A single tool call can return 100K tokens — a long code file, a thousand-row database result, a 50-page web page. Left unchecked, one tool result can fill your entire context window. Truncation is the discipline of keeping tool results useful but bounded.

The truncation toolkit

Length-based

  • Hard cap: truncate to N tokens; append [...truncated...].
  • Head/tail: keep first K and last K tokens; drop the middle.
  • Top-N lines: for structured output, keep first and last N lines.

Structure-based

  • Keep schema, sample rows: for DB results, return schema + first 10 rows + count.
  • Keep signatures: for code, return function signatures + types; drop bodies.
  • Filter then return: ask the tool to return only what was asked.

Semantic

  • Re-rank: return all, but mark top-K by relevance.
  • Summarise on the fly: a small model summarises before returning.
  • Embed-then-page: store in vector DB, return pointer + summary.

Smart bounds

  • Reserve window: reserve tokens for tools in your budget (§1).
  • Iterate: page through results; don't return the entire corpus.
  • Just-in-time: store in object store, give reference, fetch on demand.

The "page through" pattern

The most production-friendly approach is to never return the whole result in one shot. Instead, the tool returns a small slice + a cursor, and the agent fetches more only if it needs to.

# A paginated search tool — returns 5 results + a cursor
def search_docs(query: str, cursor: str = None) -> dict:
    results = vector_store.search(query, top_k=5, cursor=cursor)
    return {
        "results":      results.docs,            # < 2K tokens
        "next_cursor":  results.next_cursor,
        "total":        results.total_count,    # "5,234 more"
    }

# Agent can call search_docs again with the new cursor
# if it wants to see more — bounded cost, iterative exploration.

Where to truncate

Return the high-signal bits (summary, top slice, schema) and a pointer for the rest. Let the agent pull more if needed.
Don't truncate code blindly. Truncating a function body mid-line breaks parsing. Truncating a JSON result mid-object breaks the parser. Use structure-aware truncation: stop at function boundaries, object closing braces, line endings. Better yet, ask the tool to do the truncation at the source.[13]
📏Layman's versionplain English, no jargon

Imagine asking a librarian for books about "climate change" and them dumping 1,000 full books on your desk.

That's what happens when an AI tool returns a huge result — a 50-page PDF, a thousand-row database query, an entire codebase. One tool call and your AI's desk is overflowing. So you truncate: pick the parts that matter, return only those, and let the AI ask for more if it needs to.

Four ways to do it:

1. Length-based: "give me the first 2,000 words" or "give me the first and last 50 lines." Crude but works for prose.
2. Structure-based: "give me the table schema + first 10 rows + 'plus 5,234 more rows.'" Way better for databases — the AI sees the shape of the data, gets samples, and knows where to find the rest.
3. Semantic: "give me the top 5 results ranked by relevance to what I'm trying to do." Best for search-style queries.
4. Just-in-time: don't return it. Save it to a file, give it a reference number. If the AI wants to look something up, it asks for the file. The desk stays clean.

The killer pattern: pagination. Don't return the whole search result. Return 5 items + a cursor ("here are the next 5 if you need them"). The AI reads the first 5, decides if it needs more, and asks. This is how Google works, how SQL queries work, how every well-designed search API works.

The landmine: don't truncate code or JSON in the middle of a structure. Cutting a function body mid-line, or a JSON object mid-brace, breaks the parser. Truncate at boundaries — function ends, JSON closing braces, the end of a logical block.

The librarian analogy: when you ask for books on a topic, a good librarian doesn't dump a thousand books on your desk. They hand you the 5 most relevant, tell you "we have 200 more if you need them," and offer to pull specific books on request. Tool truncation works the same way: return the high-signal bits, plus a pointer for the rest. The desk stays clean; the AI stays in control.

Lost in the Middle & mitigation

Liu et al. (2023) showed a robust phenomenon: language models attend strongly to information at the beginning and end of their input, and perform close to random for information buried in the middle. The longer the context, the worse the middle. This single finding reframes how you should structure prompts.

The evidence

Liu et al. inserted random "needle" facts at varying positions in a long context and measured retrieval accuracy. Result: U-shaped performance curve — recall is high at the start, collapses in the middle, recovers at the end. The effect is worse for longer contexts and weaker models, but persists even in frontier models with 100K+ context windows.[1]

The U-curve: retrieval accuracy vs. position. Endpoints are reliable; the middle is risky. Place critical information at start or end.

Why does it happen?

  • → Attention is biased by position. Transformers (without relative position encodings) have weaker attention to tokens far from the query token at the end. Long-context extensions (RoPE, ALiBi, YaRN) help but don't fully fix it.
  • → Training distribution. Most pre-training data has shorter contexts; models didn't learn to integrate information across long distances.
  • → Positional primacy & recency. There's evidence models weight the first and last positions more heavily — the same bias that affects humans.

Mitigation tactics

1. Sandwich the critical content

Place the most important instructions and retrieved facts at the start of the system message and repeat them at the end of the user message. Hit both attention peaks.

2. Re-order retrieved docs

For multi-document RAG, place the most relevant doc at the start AND the end. Put mid-relevance docs in the middle (where they won't be relied on as heavily).

3. Trim aggressively

Shorter context > longer context for recall. The middle problem is exacerbated at 50K+ tokens. If you can fit in 8K, do.

4. Use structured delimiters

Wrap critical content in <important>…</important> tags. Strong models pay special attention to marked regions.

5. Re-query when uncertain

For RAG, design the system to: (1) read docs, (2) if answer not found, re-query with the partial answer as context. Two shorter queries beat one long one.

6. Explicit "answer here" markers

End the prompt with: "Based on the above, your answer:" — recent papers show that forcing the model to "look back" before answering helps retrieval from the middle.

The empirical reality check

Follow-up work (RULER, 2024; Summary-of-a-Haystack, 2024; BABILong, 2024) refined the picture:[6][5]

  • The U-curve flattens with better models and longer-context training, but never fully goes away.
  • Variable-length tasks (retrieval over changing input sizes) are harder than fixed-length.
  • Multi-hop reasoning across long contexts remains an open problem.
  • RAG + long-context is complementary, not redundant.
Practical rule: if a fact needs to be reliably recalled, put it in the first 10% or the last 10% of the context. If you can't, repeat it at both ends. If neither works, the model genuinely can't handle that fact — use a smaller context, or external retrieval, or a different architecture.[14]
🕳️Layman's versionplain English, no jargon

Imagine an audience watching a 3-hour presentation. They remember the first 5 minutes and the last 5 minutes perfectly. The middle 2.5 hours? Mostly forgotten.

That's exactly what happens with AI models. They pay strong attention to the start of their input (the "system prompt + your first recall") and the end of their input (your question + recent context). The middle 50% of the text? It's still in their head, but they basically can't reach for it when asked.

This was a big research discovery in 2023. It means that where you put information in a prompt matters as much as what information you put.

Six ways to dodge the problem:

1. Sandwich it: put the most important stuff at the very top of your prompt AND repeat it at the very bottom. Hit both attention peaks.
2. Re-order documents: if you retrieved 10 documents, put the most relevant at the start and the end, the least relevant in the middle.
3. Cut shorter: shorter prompts = better recall. If you can fit it in 8K tokens, don't use 50K.
4. Mark the important: wrap critical info in <important>…</important> tags. Strong models pay attention to marked regions.
5. Re-query when unsure: if the AI doesn't find the answer in one pass, ask it to look again with what it learned. Two short passes beat one long one.
6. Add an "answer here" prompt: end the prompt with "based on the above, your answer:" — this nudges the AI to look back over the whole context one more time before responding.

The brutal truth: newer models handle long context better than 2023's, but the U-shape never fully disappears. For really important facts, don't bury them in the middle. Put them at the top, at the bottom, or both.

The lecture analogy: your AI is a student listening to a 3-hour lecture while taking notes. They pay sharp attention at the start (settling in), drift off in the middle, and tune back in for the conclusion. If you want them to recall a specific fact, don't put it in minute 90 of a 180-minute lecture. Put it in minute 5 or minute 175. Or say it twice, at both ends.
